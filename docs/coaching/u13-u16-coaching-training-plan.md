# U13–U16 Coaching & Training Plan

> **Sport:** Association football
> **Age range:** 13–16 (roughly U14 → U17 squads depending on cut-off date)
> **Format:** 3 training sessions + 1 match per week, 40-week season
> **Written for:** the coach who runs the session, not the sports-science department

---

## Part 0 — Read this first

Three things drive everything else in this document.

**1. Chronological age is nearly useless at this stage.**
In any U14 group you will have a 12-year-old body and a 16-year-old body wearing the same bib. Two players born eleven months apart can differ by 30cm and 25kg. Planning by birth year and then wondering why half the squad can't cope is the single most common mistake at this age. Plan by **maturation**, coach by **individual**.

**2. Peak Height Velocity (PHV) is the axis the whole plan turns on.**
PHV is the period of fastest growth — typically **~11.5–13 for girls, ~13–14.5 for boys**, with huge individual variation. Around PHV:
- Bones lengthen faster than muscle and tendon adapt → players get temporarily *less* flexible, *less* coordinated, and *more* injury-prone. This is "adolescent awkwardness," it's normal, and it's not laziness.
- Previously graceful players suddenly look clumsy. Tell them this is coming, *before* it happens, or they will conclude they've got worse and quit.
- Growth-plate (apophyseal) tissue is the weak link, not muscle. Hence the knee/heel pain epidemic in this bracket.

**3. Teenagers are not small adults, and they are not big children.**
Their reward system is running hot, their impulse control is still wiring, and their sensitivity to peer status is at its lifetime maximum. That is not a problem to suppress — it's an engine to point in a useful direction. Every engagement technique in Part 5 is built on it.

> **Scope note:** This is a coaching plan, not medical advice. Anything involving pain, suspected injury, disordered eating, or a player who looks physically or psychologically at risk goes to a qualified medical professional. Strength work should be introduced under someone competent to coach technique.

## Part 1 — Maturation bands

Group your squad into three bands. Reassess every 10–12 weeks (a simple standing-height measurement each block will tell you who's in a growth spurt — **>0.5cm/month is a spurt**).

### Band A — Pre-PHV / early growth (typically 12.5–14)
Still relatively coordinated, low absolute strength, high trainability of **skill and speed**.

- **Priority:** technique under increasing pressure, agility, change of direction, running mechanics.
- **Strength:** bodyweight only. Movement quality is the goal, not load. Push-up, squat, lunge, hinge, hang, crawl, jump-and-land.
- **What to protect:** don't chase physical outcomes. Their bodies aren't ready and you'll teach them that football is about size.
- **Common trap:** the biggest early developer dominates and gets over-selected. He is not the best player; he is the earliest. The late developers are the ones who've been forced to solve problems with brain instead of body — protect them or you lose your best future players.

### Band B — Circa-PHV (the danger zone, typically 13–15)
Growing fastest. Highest injury risk of any period in a footballer's life.

- **Priority:** re-learn movement in a new body. Landing mechanics, deceleration, hip control, single-leg stability.
- **Strength:** maintain, don't push. Emphasise eccentric control and tendon tolerance.
- **What to protect:** **load**. A player in a spurt should have training volume *reduced*, not increased, and high-volume jumping/sprinting managed carefully. Watch for:
  - **Osgood-Schlatter** — pain/lump at the top of the shin below the kneecap
  - **Sinding-Larsen-Johansson** — pain at the bottom tip of the kneecap
  - **Sever's** — heel pain, worse on hard ground and in studs
  - **Hip/pelvis apophysitis** — groin or front-of-hip pain on sprinting or kicking
  These are growth-plate irritations, not "tightness." The answer is load management, not stretching through it.
- **Common trap:** benching a player because they've "lost it." They haven't. Tell them the timeline. Keep them playing.

### Band C — Post-PHV (typically 14.5–16+)
Growth slowing, hormonal environment now supports real strength and power adaptation.

- **Priority:** strength, power, repeated-sprint ability, and the tactical layer — this is when the game genuinely opens up cognitively.
- **Strength:** structured resistance training becomes highly productive. Progressive external load, technique-first, supervised.
- **What to protect:** ego lifting, and the temptation to train them like a first team. They still need skill volume.

## Part 2 — Season structure (macrocycle)

| Block | Weeks | Physical focus | Technical/tactical focus | Character focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Pre-season** | 1–6 | Movement screen, aerobic base, landing/decel mechanics, intro to strength | 1v1 mastery, first touch under pressure | Standards & team charter |
| **Early season** | 7–16 | Speed & acceleration, strength build | Attacking principles: width, depth, penetration | Handling adversity, refereeing respect |
| **Mid season** | 17–26 | Strength maintain, repeated-sprint ability | Defending principles: press triggers, cover/balance, transitions | Leadership rotation, accountability |
| **Late season** | 27–36 | Power & maximum speed | Game management, set pieces, positional specificity | Peer coaching, mentoring younger squads |
| **Off-season** | 37–40 | Deload, other sports encouraged | Individual challenge programme | Reflection, goal-setting for next season |

**Two non-negotiables across the year:**
1. **A deload week every 4th week.** ~50% volume, keep intensity, add a fun/futsal night. Teenagers under-report fatigue; you have to schedule the rest for them.
2. **Play other sports.** Early specialisation raises injury and burnout risk and doesn't improve the odds of making it. Basketball and athletics in particular transfer beautifully.

## Part 3 — The training week (microcycle)

Assuming Saturday match. Adjust names, keep the shape.

| Day | Session | Intensity | Physical emphasis | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sun** | Off / active recovery | — | — | Non-negotiable |
| **Mon** | Off or optional individual | Low | — | Recovery from match |
| **Tue** | **Session 1 — High load** | High | Strength + high-speed running | Furthest from match; body can absorb it |
| **Wed** | Off | — | — | 48h to recover |
| **Thu** | **Session 2 — Technical/tactical** | Moderate | Change of direction, agility | Skill-dense, moderate physical cost |
| **Fri** | **Session 3 — Sharp & short** (or off) | Low-moderate | Activation, speed, set pieces | Primes the nervous system, no fatigue |
| **Sat** | **Match** | Max | — | The point of the week |

**Session 3 is the one coaches get wrong.** It should be 50 minutes, high-quality, low-volume, and finish with players wanting more. Long, heavy Friday sessions cost you Saturday.

**Timing matters.** Adolescents have a genuinely delayed body clock — the sleep-phase shift at puberty is biological, not attitude. A 7am session gets you a physiologically half-asleep group. Evening sessions get you real output. If you must train early, extend the warm-up significantly.

## Part 4 — Session architecture (the 90-minute template)

This template is the backbone. It's designed so the ball is rolling and players are deciding for as much of the session as possible.

| Phase | Time | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **0. Arrival activity** | 10 min (pre-start) | Ball out, small-sided game, no coach input. Late arrivals join seamlessly. |
| **1. Warm-up / injury prevention** | 15 min | Structured neuromuscular programme (Part 6). Player-led rota. |
| **2. Skill block** | 15 min | One clear technical theme, high repetition, opposed as early as possible |
| **3. Game-related practice** | 20 min | The theme inside a directional game with real constraints |
| **4. Game training / SSG** | 25 min | Full context, minimal interruption |
| **5. Free game or challenge** | 10 min | Player-chosen format. Coach shuts up. |
| **6. Cool-down + huddle** | 5 min | 3 sentences max. One thing done well, one thing to work on, one thing tomorrow. |

### The delivery rules that make it work

**Ball-rolling time > 60%.** Time the session once. Actually time it. Most coaches discover they're at 35% and the rest is queuing, walking, and talking. Fixing this single number transforms engagement more than any drill you'll ever find.

**No lines, no laps, no lectures.** Three or more players waiting for a turn means the practice design is wrong. Split into more grids, more balls, smaller groups.

**The 30-second rule.** Any explanation over 30 seconds loses the group. Demonstrate rather than describe. Set the practice going, then coach individuals inside it. If you need 3 minutes to explain it, the practice is too complicated.

**Freeze sparingly — 2 or 3 times a session, maximum.** Each freeze is a debt you pay in momentum. Use "coaching in the flow" instead: walk alongside a player during a natural break and ask one question.

**Ask, don't tell.** "What did you see?" / "What were your options there?" / "What would you do differently?" Guided discovery is slower in week 1 and dramatically faster by week 10, because they can solve it without you on Saturday. A player who only knows what you told them is a player who freezes when you're not shouting.

**Change the constraints, not the instructions.** If you want more passing, make the pitch wider or add a two-touch limit — don't yell "pass it!" for 20 minutes. The environment teaches better than your voice does. This is the single highest-leverage coaching habit on this list.

Examples of constraints that teach themselves:
| You want... | Constraint |
|---|---|
| Quicker decisions | Two-touch, or a 6-second shot clock |
| Playing forward | Goal counts double if scored within 8s of winning possession |
| Scanning before receiving | Coach holds up fingers behind the play; receiver must call the number |
| Width | Wide channels only enterable by wingers; goals from crosses count double |
| Pressing as a unit | Defending team wins a point for every turnover in the attacking third |
| Weak-foot development | Goals with the weak foot count double |
| Composure in the box | One-touch finish zone |

## Part 5 — Engagement: how to actually hold a teenager's attention

This is the part most plans skip. A perfect periodised programme delivered badly gets ignored. Here is the deep version.

### 5.1 The three psychological needs

Intrinsic motivation — the kind that survives you not being there — is built on three things. Every technique below serves one of them.

**Autonomy — "I have a say."**
Teenagers are in the business of separating from adult authority. That's developmentally correct behaviour, not defiance. A coach who controls everything triggers exactly the resistance they're trying to avoid.
- **Player's choice finisher.** The last 10 minutes belongs to them. World Cup, crossbar challenge, 3v3 tournament, whatever. Non-negotiably theirs.
- **Warm-up rota.** Each week a different pair leads the warm-up. They have to know it well enough to teach it — which means they learn it properly. Best injury-prevention compliance tool that exists.
- **Choice within structure.** "You're doing finishing today. Do you want it from the cross or from the cutback?" Two options, both of which you're happy with. The choice is real; the outcome is controlled.
- **Ask them to design a practice.** Once a block. They will design something too complicated, it will fail, and they will suddenly understand why you set things up the way you do.

**Competence — "I'm getting better and I can see it."**
Teenagers quit things they think they're bad at. The perception matters more than the reality.
- **Make progress visible.** Personal bests, not team averages. Wall chart, app, whiteboard — anything they can point at. Juggling record, 5m sprint, weak-foot passes in 60s, Nordic hold time.
- **Compete against yourself.** Every measurable challenge is *your last score*, not the best player's score. Otherwise the same kid wins every week and everyone else disengages by October.
- **Challenge ladders.** Bronze/Silver/Gold versions of each skill challenge, so every player is working at the edge of their ability rather than bored or humiliated.
- **Name drills after players.** "Right, we're doing the Amir turn." Costs nothing. Enormous status payoff. They will practise it in the garden.
- **5:1 feedback ratio.** Five specific positives per correction. Specific — "the way you opened your body before you received, that's exactly it" beats "well done." Generic praise reads as insincere to a 15-year-old and they will file you accordingly.

**Relatedness — "I belong here."**
The strongest retention predictor at this age is not enjoyment of the sport. It's friendship within the team.
- **Rotate small-group pairings deliberately.** Left to themselves they'll stick with their two mates all season and the squad fractures into cliques.
- **Protect the late developers publicly.** The smallest 13-year-old is watching how you treat him for a signal about whether he has a future here.
- **Learn what they're into.** Two minutes of non-football conversation per player per week. Not a technique — just be a person. It buys you enormous latitude when you need to be demanding.
- **Rituals.** A pre-match huddle phrase, a song, a handshake. Cringeworthy to adults, load-bearing for teenagers.

### 5.2 Practical engagement toolkit

**Competition, constantly.** The adolescent reward system is primed for it. Almost anything becomes engaging when scored: leagues within the session, running totals across a block, first-to-5, king of the court, losers reset the cones. Keep the stakes light and the scoreboard visible.

**Music in the warm-up.** Player-curated playlist, one rule about lyrics, rotate the DJ. It changes the arrival atmosphere completely — players show up early to influence the playlist.

**Phones — use them, don't fight them.** Ban them and you've picked a war you'll lose. Instead:
- 60-second clips from Saturday, cut and dropped into the group chat with one coaching point
- Players film each other's technique in pairs — they see things in themselves they'd never accept from you
- A weekly individual challenge posted to the group, with video proof
- Rule: phones in the bag during the session, out for the last 10 minutes if they've earned it

**Vary the format.** Futsal night. Rondo tournament. Coaches vs players. Blindfolded communication drills. Play with a size 3 ball. Play in the rain deliberately and make it an event. Novelty is not frivolous — it's how you avoid the mid-season plateau where attendance quietly drops.

**Give real responsibility.** Captain rotates weekly. A player runs the set-piece meeting. The U16s coach a U9 session — nothing makes a 15-year-old grow up faster than 20 nine-year-olds not listening to them.

**Manage banter actively.** The default norm in a teenage male group is savage mockery, and it will silently destroy the confidence of the players who need it most. The standard to set: **play the ball, not the player.** Mock the miss, never the person. Say it out loud, in week one, and enforce it consistently, including on your best player — *especially* on your best player, because that's the moment everyone finds out whether the standard is real.

**Watch what you praise.** Praise effort, decision-making, and recovery runs. If you only celebrate goals, you'll get a squad that only wants to score.

### 5.3 Handling the hard moments

| Situation | What not to do | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Player sulking after being subbed | Public confrontation | Let them cool. Speak 1:1 after. "I get why you're annoyed. Here's why, and here's what gets you the next 90." |
| Group won't focus | Shout louder | Change the activity. Non-focus is almost always a design problem, not a discipline problem. |
| Player in a growth spurt losing form | Drop them | Name it explicitly. "Your body's growing faster than your coordination. This is temporary and every player goes through it." |
| Heavy defeat | Post-match teardown | Say almost nothing. One sentence. Review Tuesday when the emotion's gone. Emotional arousal blocks learning. |
| Dissent at the referee | Ignore it | Immediate, consistent, no exceptions. See Part 7. |
| Star player disengaged | Give more attention | Give more *challenge*. Boredom looks identical to attitude at this age. |

## Part 6 — Physical development: what you're building and why

### 6.1 The injury-prevention warm-up (15 min, every session, non-negotiable)

Structured neuromuscular warm-up programmes — the FIFA 11+ family and similar — have consistently been shown to substantially reduce injury rates in youth football, particularly ACL and hamstring injuries. Compliance is the whole game: the effect appears in teams that do it *properly, twice a week or more*. Half-hearted delivery gets you nothing.

**Part 1 — Running (5 min)**
Straight-line jogging, hip out, hip in, circling partner, shoulder contact, quick forward/back.
*Building:* aerobic prep, hip mobility, controlled contact tolerance.

**Part 2 — Strength / plyometrics / balance (7 min)**
The core content. Progress through three levels over the season.

| Exercise | Primary muscles | Why it matters in football |
|---|---|---|
| **Nordic hamstring curl** | Hamstrings (esp. biceps femoris long head), eccentrically | The most evidence-backed hamstring-injury reducer available. Sprint deceleration, the moment the hamstring tears. |
| **Copenhagen adduction** | Adductor longus/brevis/magnus | Groin injury is one of football's most common and most chronic problems. Direct protection. Start with short-lever. |
| **Single-leg RDL** | Glute max, hamstrings, deep hip stabilisers | Football is a single-leg sport. Every stride, every strike is on one leg. |
| **Side plank / hip abduction** | Glute medius, QL, obliques | Controls the knee collapsing inward — the ACL mechanism. Also shielding and duels. |
| **Plank variations, anti-rotation** | Deep trunk, transverse abdominis, obliques | Transfers force from ground to ball, and keeps you upright when someone hits you. |
| **Squat & lunge patterns** | Quads, glutes, adductors | Foundation of every acceleration and every jump. |
| **Calf raises (bent and straight knee)** | Gastrocnemius, **soleus** | The soleus takes several times bodyweight per stride at sprint. Massively under-trained. Protects Achilles and helps Sever's. |
| **Jump-and-land / hop-and-stick** | Whole lower chain, eccentrically | Landing mechanics. Teach knees-over-toes-not-inward, soft, quiet, absorbed through the hip. **This is the single most important item on this list for ACL risk.** |

**Part 3 — Speed & sharpness (3 min)**
Bounding, plant-and-cut, sprints across the pitch. Full effort, full recovery.

**Delivery note:** run it as a competition or a player-led circuit with music. Delivered as a boring compliance exercise it will be quietly sabotaged within a month.

### 6.2 Strength training by band

The idea that resistance training stunts growth is a myth with no support in the evidence. Supervised, technique-first resistance training is safe and beneficial for adolescents. The real risks are poor supervision, ego-driven maximal attempts, and bad technique under load.

| Band | Approach | Load | Session length |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A (pre-PHV)** | Movement mastery, bodyweight, medicine ball, play-based | Bodyweight | 15 min, 2×/week, tacked onto training |
| **B (circa-PHV)** | Maintain. Eccentric control, tendon tolerance, single-leg | Bodyweight + light external | 20 min, 2×/week, reduce if in spurt |
| **C (post-PHV)** | Progressive resistance, real strength adaptation | Progressive external load | 30–40 min, 2×/week |

**Fundamental patterns to cover regardless of band:** squat, hinge, lunge, push, pull, carry, rotate, jump, land, brace.

**The commonly neglected areas, and why they cost you matches:**
- **Posterior chain (glutes + hamstrings)** — teenagers are overwhelmingly quad-dominant. This imbalance is directly implicated in both hamstring strains and ACL risk. Prioritise it.
- **Adductors** — groin problems become chronic and ruin seasons. Cheap to prevent, expensive to fix.
- **Soleus** — under-trained, enormous loads in sprinting.
- **Upper body and grip** — at 15–16 the physical duel becomes decisive. Players who can't hold their ground get bullied out of games. Rows, pull-ups/hangs, push-ups, carries.
- **Neck** — relevant for heading exposure; noting that heading should be limited at this age per current guidance in many federations. **Check and follow your national association's heading rules** — they vary and they've changed recently.

### 6.3 Speed, agility and conditioning

- **Sprint work goes early in the session**, when fresh. Speed is a skill of the nervous system, not a fitness quality — trained tired, you're just teaching them to run slowly.
- Football is repeated short efforts with incomplete recovery. **Small-sided games are excellent conditioning** and cost you nothing in skill time. Running laps is a waste of a teenager's session.
- **Deceleration is undertrained and does more damage than acceleration.** Braking forces are where knees fail. Coach stopping as deliberately as you coach starting.
- **Change of direction under a reactive cue** (a defender's movement, a coach's call) beats pre-planned cone weaving. The game never tells you where you're going in advance.

### 6.4 Sleep, food, load — the boring stuff that decides everything

Say this to the group repeatedly and make it a standard, not a lecture:
- **Sleep 8–10 hours.** It is the highest-return performance intervention available to a teenager and the one they're all failing. Injury risk climbs measurably with chronic short sleep.
- **Eat properly and eat enough.** Growing bodies plus football have high energy requirements. Under-fuelling in this bracket causes stalled growth, stress fractures, and hormonal problems — and it affects both boys and girls.
- **Hydrate.**
- **Track total load, not just your sessions.** Your three sessions plus a match plus school PE plus a district team plus a Sunday club is how a 14-year-old ends up with a stress fracture. Ask what else they're doing. Actually ask.
- **Report pain early.** Build a culture where saying "my knee hurts" is treated as intelligent, not soft. The macho alternative turns a two-week problem into a six-month one.

## Part 7 — Sportsmanship and character

Treat this as a coached curriculum with the same seriousness as finishing. It is the part of the programme that still matters when they've stopped playing.

### 7.1 The team charter (do this in week one)

Don't hand them rules. Facilitate them writing their own, in their own words — you'll only need to steer it slightly, and they will enforce it on each other in a way they never enforce yours. Cover: how we treat referees, how we treat teammates who make mistakes, how we react to losing, what "trying hard" looks like, what we do in the group chat. Print it. Put it on the wall. Revisit it when it's broken.

### 7.2 What you're building, block by block

| Block | Trait | How you actually coach it |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-season | **Respect & standards** | Charter. Set-up/pack-down rota. Handshake with the referee before and after, every game, no exceptions. |
| Early season | **Resilience** | Deliberately unfair games — 8v11, concede a 2-goal head start, award a bad decision against them. Then debrief the *reaction*, not the result. This is rehearsal for Saturday. |
| Mid season | **Accountability** | Rotating captaincy. Player-led halftime for 2 minutes before you speak. Own your error out loud, then move on. |
| Late season | **Leadership & empathy** | Senior players mentor a younger squad. Peer coaching pairs. |
| Throughout | **Humility in winning** | Coach how to win properly as hard as you coach how to lose properly. No showboating at 5–0. Applaud the opposition. |

### 7.3 Referee respect — the one to be absolutely rigid about

Most referees at youth level are teenagers themselves, and abuse is why they quit. Set the standard once and never bend it:
- **Only the captain speaks to the referee.** Everyone else, silence.
- **Dissent = immediate substitution.** Every time, every player, including your best one. The first time you apply it to your star is the moment the rule becomes real.
- **Referee your own games in training.** Nothing generates empathy like being the one making the calls while your mates argue.
- **Model it yourself.** They copy what you do on the touchline, not what you say in the huddle. If you scream at officials, nothing above matters.

### 7.4 Parents

Brief them at the start of the season and be direct: their job on the touchline is **encouragement only** — no coaching, no refereeing, no criticising other people's children. Conflicting instructions from the touchline is the most common cause of teenage paralysis on a pitch. Give them a clear line and give them a job (kit, transport, filming), because occupied parents are quiet parents.

## Part 8 — Measuring progress

Measure a small number of things, consistently, and show players the trend. Visible progress is the engine of competence, which is the engine of motivation.

**Physical** (each block, ~30 min, run as a competition)
- 10m and 30m sprint
- Countermovement jump
- Nordic hold or eccentric hamstring measure
- Copenhagen hold time
- Yo-Yo IR1 or similar
- **Standing height** — the growth-spurt tracker. Non-negotiable, it's how you assign bands.

**Technical** (each block)
- Weak-foot passes to target in 60s
- Juggling PB
- 1v1 success rate in a standard practice
- First-touch under pressure, scored

**Match** (each game)
- Something individual and non-scoreboard for every player. A defender's "duels won." A midfielder's "forward passes completed." A striker's "presses that forced a turnover." Every player leaves with a number that is theirs and that isn't goals.

**Character** (each block)
- Self-assessment against the team charter, then a coach conversation about the gap. The conversation is the intervention.

> Reporting these back individually and privately — rather than as a public league table — is what keeps the bottom third of the squad engaged. Team-visible rankings are a great way to lose your late developers by Christmas.

## Part 10 — Coach's weekly checklist

- [ ] Did I know which maturation band every player was in this week?
- [ ] Was the ball rolling more than 60% of the session?
- [ ] Did I speak for less than 10% of the session?
- [ ] Did I ask more questions than I gave answers?
- [ ] Did every player get at least one specific, individual piece of positive feedback?
- [ ] Did the injury-prevention warm-up get done properly — not rushed, not skipped?
- [ ] Did I have a genuine non-football conversation with at least three players?
- [ ] Did I enforce the referee standard, including on my best player?
- [ ] Did anyone report pain, and did I respond well enough that they'll report it again next time?
- [ ] Did the players leave wanting the next session?

## Appendix — Quick reference

**Growth-spurt warning signs:** >0.5cm/month height gain · sudden clumsiness · knee/heel/hip pain · loss of previously reliable technique · fatigue disproportionate to load.
→ Reduce volume, protect landing/jumping load, tell the player what's happening, keep them playing.

**The four biggest mistakes at U13–U16:**
1. Coaching the birth year instead of the body.
2. Talking too much and letting the ball sit still.
3. Selecting the early developers and losing the late ones.
4. Adding load to a player who is growing fastest, because they "look strong now."

**If you only change one thing:** time your ball-rolling percentage next session, then fix it. Everything else in this document gets easier once that number is right.
